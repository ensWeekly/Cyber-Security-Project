REFERENCE SHELF

Understand it. Then use it.

Open a concept
Encryption & keys

Encryption transforms readable plaintext into ciphertext using an algorithm and a key. Decryption restores it with the appropriate key. With symmetric encryption, the same secret key is used to encrypt and decrypt. Keep secret keys out of the message channel.

A Caesar shift has only 25 non-zero keys. An attacker can try them all. It is a learning model, not real security. Modern systems need well-tested cryptography and secure key management.

NIST: encryption ↗
TLS or end-to-end?

HTTPS uses TLS to protect data between a browser and a server. The service can usually read it after decryption. End-to-end encryption is designed so message contents are readable only at the endpoints, not by the relay service.

Neither prevents a recipient taking a screenshot or protects an unlocked, compromised device. Encryption in transit does not automatically protect stored data. Encryption alone does not establish that a request is honest.

Cloudflare: HTTPS ↗
Permissions & breaches

Give access only to people who need it. Avoid public links. Use strong unique passwords, MFA and independent identity checks. Collect the minimum data and decide when to delete it.

If exposure happens: restrict the exposed link or account, report promptly to the school IT/safeguarding contact, preserve relevant evidence securely, and follow school procedures. Do not forward private content to prove a breach.

CISA: safer online habits ↗
Anonymity & digital choices

Anonymity can help someone ask for support without public embarrassment. It can also make impersonation or harmful messages harder to investigate. A private identity check by authorised staff can support accountability without public disclosure.

Consider consent, purpose and harm before sharing. Copies and screenshots can persist after a post is deleted. School rules and applicable privacy requirements still matter; this task asks you to reason ethically, not give legal advice.

TEACHER GUIDE

Two lessons.
Observable understanding.

Securing the Digital World: Exploring Encryption and Cybersecurity

Before the lessons

  1. Open the site on student devices. Prepare paper for cipher working.
  2. Allocate anonymous student codes and partner codes. Use one case per student or distribute A–C.
  3. Specify the school platform and deadline for PDF submission. Students do not need GitHub accounts.
  4. Check each student can export a backup. On iPad use Share/Print or the browser’s print options to save the report.
  5. Keep student answers out of the public repository.

Limit AI through task design

  • Class-only working time and a handwritten cipher.
  • Take a first-draft screenshot/checkpoint before revision.
  • Partner code and specific observed outcomes for both tests.
  • Use a brief oral check during work. Sample any answer that needs verification.
  • AI only for teacher-approved spelling/grammar, with the original retained and use declared. No automated detection claims or blocked-paste gimmicks.
Checkpoint prompts & manageable verification

Lesson 1: circulate during minutes 15–25 and 50–55. Ask: “Show how you changed this letter” and “Who can read it at this point?” Record observations in your own class list.

Lesson 2: circulate during tests and revisions as well as minutes 45–53. Use a 20–30 second check per student; larger classes should start these checks while students work. Give one fresh constraint: “The recipient forwards a screenshot”, “The key is stolen”, or “Your user loses the device.” Ask the student to adapt one rule and explain the limit.

This is supervised assessment, not exam-lockdown software. This page and its instructions are public; teacher observations and marks belong in the school’s assessment system.

Support & extension

Support: allow a drawn flow and handwritten answers instead of typing; use a teacher-approved scribe where needed. Give a printed alphabet strip and rehearse the first word together. Students may dictate an oral explanation under normal school accommodations. Assess the same cybersecurity understanding.

Sentence starters: “Only ___ should read this because ___.” “Encryption helps when ___, but cannot stop ___.” “Our test showed ___, so I changed ___.”

Extension: justify TLS vs end-to-end encryption, identify metadata left exposed, or explain why encryption needs authentication and integrity controls.
